## Introducción
**Should**, **must** y **have to** expresan consejo y obligación. Se parecen, pero no son iguales, y sus negativas significan cosas muy distintas.

## Explicación central
### should: consejo, recomendación
- *You **should** drink more water.* (te lo aconsejo) — *You **shouldn't** eat so much sugar.*
- Pregunta: ***Should** I call her?* (¿te parece que la llame?)

### must: obligación fuerte (del que habla o de una norma)
- *I **must** finish this today.* (lo siento como obligación propia)
- *Visitors **must** sign in at reception.* (norma escrita)
- **mustn't** = **prohibido**: *You **mustn't** smoke here.*

### have to: obligación externa
- *I **have to** wear a uniform at work.* (me lo exige la empresa)
- Se conjuga: *she **has to**, they **had to**, we **will have to***. Negativa y pregunta con *do*: *Do you have to work on Saturdays?*
- **don't have to** = **no hace falta** (no es prohibido): *You **don't have to** come if you're busy.*

### Pasado y futuro
- *Must* no tiene pasado: se usa ***had to***: *Yesterday I **had to** stay late.*
- Futuro: ***will have to***: *You'll have to wait.*

## Errores comunes de hispanohablantes
1. **Confundir *mustn't* y *don't have to***: ❌ *You mustn't pay; it's free.* ✅ *You don't have to pay; it's free.*
2. **Agregar *to* a should/must**: ❌ *You should to study.* ✅ *You should study.*
3. ❌ *I musted go.* ✅ *I had to go.*
4. ❌ *She have to work.* ✅ *She has to work.*

## Comparación con el español
«Deberías» = *should*; «tengo que» = *have to* (o *must*). La trampa está en la negativa: «no tenés que pagar» (no hace falta) = *you don't have to pay*, mientras que *you mustn't pay* sería «no debés pagar / está prohibido pagar».

## Ejemplo extendido en contexto
*At my new job I **have to** start at 8, and we **mustn't** use our phones in meetings. Tomorrow is a holiday, so I **don't have to** go. My friend says I **should** ask for a later start time — but last week I **had to** stay until 9!*
